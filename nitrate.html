Nitrate

Measuring nitrate

Why it's regulated, why it's hard to measure in the field, and how Spectril is designed to measure it.

Why it's regulated

Nitrate reaches groundwater and streams from fertilizer, manure and septic systems. EPA's limit in public drinking water is 10 mg/L as nitrogen, set mainly to protect infants. The EU and WHO use 50 mg/L as nitrate, which is about 11.3 mg/L as nitrogen.

Private wells aren't covered by federal drinking water rules, so owners are responsible for their own testing.

Watch the units

Nitrate is reported two ways, and the numbers differ by a factor of about 4.4. Spectril reports nitrate as nitrogen, the way EPA states its limit.

Why it's hard to measure

  • The light is deep UVNitrate absorbs most strongly near 200 nm. Light sources at these wavelengths are scarce: no credible 220 nm LED was found, and 235 nm is the shortest Spectril could confirm is sold.
  • The signal sits on a steep slopeAt 235 nm nitrate's absorption falls by about 20% per nanometre. An LED that warms up or ages shifts slightly in wavelength, and the reading moves with it.
  • Other things absorb there tooNitrite absorbs in the same region and is counted as nitrate. Dissolved organic matter adds a background, and particles scatter light. Iodide, bisulfide and bromide also interfere.
  • Electrodes and strips have their own limitsNitrate electrodes respond to other ions, such as chloride, and need frequent calibration. Test strips are read by eye against a colour chart in coarse steps.

How Spectril measures it

The method is UV absorbance at two wavelengths, the same approach used by YSI's commercial NitraLED sensor. Spectril adds a reference detector, a repeat reading, an audit record and, optionally, a Raman check.

  1. Filter. The sample goes through a 0.45 µm syringe filter, which removes most particles. Nothing is added to it.
  2. Measure at 235 nm. Nitrate absorbs here. At the 10 mg/L limit, about 37% of the light is absorbed over the 10 mm path, which gives a clear signal.
  3. Measure at 275 nm. Nitrate barely absorbs here, but organic matter does. This reading is used to correct the 235 nm result.
  4. Compare with clean water. Each reading is divided by the reference diode and compared with a deionized water blank taken in the same cell.
  5. Correct and check. Temperature is corrected, and a repeat 235 nm reading confirms nothing drifted during the test.
  6. Confirm with Raman, if fitted. Nitrate has its own Raman peak at 1046 cm⁻¹, separate from nitrite, so a high UV reading can be checked against it. Whether the low-cost camera can see it at 10 mg/L is still untested.

How results are reported

Every result is a range, based on the measured uncertainty.

  • Above limitThe whole range is above 10 mg/L.
  • Near limitThe range crosses 10 mg/L. Spectril asks you to send a split sample to a certified lab.
  • Below limitThe whole range is below 10 mg/L.

The most important rule in testing is that no sample above 10 mg/L may be reported as clear.

Limits

  • Screening onlyUS compliance tests must be run by a certified lab using listed methods, and UV absorbance isn't on the nitrate list.
  • Nitrite is includedThe UV reading counts nitrite as nitrate. This will be disclosed with every result.
  • Fresh water onlySeawater and brackish water aren't supported, because bromide interferes.
  • Organic-rich waterThe two-wavelength correction is meant for water with little organic matter. Streams with a lot of it may read high.
